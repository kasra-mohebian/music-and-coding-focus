# Evidence briefing: music, developer focus and task performance

## The question

Music can mask an environment, regulate mood or become another demand on attention. The review asks what evidence supports each possibility during programming, while separating experience, measured task outcomes and general cognitive mechanisms.

## What is closest to programming work?

M01 is a remote-developer survey: its methods report 130 collected and 93 retained responses. It supports discussion of perceived productivity and work context, with self-selection and self-report limitations. M02 and M03 are relevant programming/developer references, but their primary full-text outcomes were not extracted in this edition. [M01](../REFERENCES.md#m01) · [M02](../REFERENCES.md#m02) · [M03](../REFERENCES.md#m03)

M04 tested EEG in several development situations. Its music pilots are very small, and sections V and VII report inconclusive music findings. The more developed pair-programming results answer a different question and should not be presented as a music benefit. [M04](../REFERENCES.md#m04)

M07 concerns 62 systems analysts and a data-modeling task. Its institutional abstract reports mood effects but no apparent treatment effect on model quality. This is adjacent evidence, checked at abstract level, that helps separate affect from output quality. [M07](../REFERENCES.md#m07)

M09/M10 evaluate a music recommender using concentration estimates, preferences and interface behavior. Their eight-student computer-work sample includes four programmers; the later paper adds evaluations, including a 25-person interface study. The authors identify direct concentration-effect measurement as future work. Neither establishes improved code correctness. M10 extends M09, so their shared evidence should not be counted twice. [M09](../REFERENCES.md#m09) · [M10](../REFERENCES.md#m10)

## What does broader cognitive research add?

M11 finds better reported task focus in a 40-student attention task without significant improvement in mean reaction time or its variability. M12 analyzes pupil data from the same underlying dataset, with 39 participants; it is not an independent replication. [M11](../REFERENCES.md#m11) · [M12](../REFERENCES.md#m12)

M13 compares tempo and emotional valence in cognitive tasks with 40 participants. Some outcomes favor faster or positive music relative to other musical conditions. It has no silence baseline, so it cannot establish that music improves performance over no music. M14 investigates perceptual learning after music listening, a different timing and outcome from background music during coding. [M13](../REFERENCES.md#m13) · [M14](../REFERENCES.md#m14)

M08 concerns calculation and adaptive volume. M15–M18 concern learning or reading. These studies can inform questions about task load and preference, but should not be treated as coding experiments. In M18, 33 graduate students showed no significant overall difference in accuracy or reading/answering time among five audio conditions; engagement differed. M17 measured perceived learning outcomes in a 30-person field study. [M08](../REFERENCES.md#m08) · [M15](../REFERENCES.md#m15) · [M16](../REFERENCES.md#m16) · [M17](../REFERENCES.md#m17) · [M18](../REFERENCES.md#m18)

M05 and M06 supply context about fragmented developer work and interruptions; neither tests a music intervention. [M05](../REFERENCES.md#m05) · [M06](../REFERENCES.md#m06)

## Practical interpretation

A reasonable personal comparison records both how focused a session feels and task outcomes, such as completed checks, correctness or comparable completion time. Vary session order and account for task difficulty and background noise. This is a proposed self-observation approach, not an experiment conducted by this project.

For teams, evidence about preference and mood does not justify a universal music rule. Communication needs, task demands and individual differences should remain part of the decision.

## Confidence and remaining work

The review offers a narrative synthesis rather than a meta-analysis. It does not substantiate a universal BPM/volume range, a fixed lyrical-music penalty or a percentage of 'positive studies.' Six references still need primary full-text access for deeper appraisal. Exact checked access, limitations and locators are in the [18 study appraisals](study-appraisals.md) and [machine-readable register](../evidence/studies.csv).
