# Review protocol

## Question and intended use

How does music during programming relate to concentration and task performance? The review aims to help developers reason about their work environment and recognize the limits of available evidence.

Subquestions concern instrumental versus lyrical music, task demands, preference/familiarity, tempo/volume, and office versus remote settings. Each requires evidence that actually measures that factor; one source should not stand in for all subquestions.

## Search plan retained from the course work

Search ACM Digital Library, with IEEE Xplore as a supplementary source, using combinations of:

```text
(developer OR programmer OR "software engineer" OR coder)
AND (music OR "background music" OR audio OR sound)
AND (concentration OR focus OR attention OR "task performance" OR productivity OR "cognitive load")
AND (programming OR coding OR debugging OR development)
```

The original intended window was 2005–2025, English-language empirical research. Bibliographic sources can help verify titles and venues. This document records that plan; it does not claim a newly rerun exhaustive database search or manufacture screening counts.

## Relevance rules

Classify each candidate before synthesis:

1. Direct developer/programming evidence: software developers or an actual programming task, with a relevant music condition or reported music practice.
2. Indirect evidence: general attention, reading, learning, calculation or workplace studies. These may motivate hypotheses but do not directly establish a coding benefit.
3. Mixed evidence: samples or evaluations combine programming and other tasks. Systems-analysis work is marked direct-adjacent rather than treated as a code-quality experiment.
4. Context only: developer productivity, interruption or environment research without a music comparison.

Record the design and source access separately. A verified citation or abstract is not the same as a full-text methods/results check. Do not count every candidate as an included direct experiment.

## Extraction and quality appraisal

For each study, record its population, sample and exclusions, task, music condition, comparator, outcome definition, effect estimate if available, uncertainty and limitations. Link every numeric claim to a table, figure, section or other exact source location. Mark unreported fields rather than filling them from inference.

Assess selection bias, self-report versus performance measurement, order effects, task equivalence, and whether the population supports transfer to professional programming. Venue reputation alone is not a sufficient quality score.

## Synthesis

Use a narrative synthesis, keeping designs and outcomes separate. Do not pool incompatible tasks, tally 'positive' papers without an auditable extraction sheet, or claim causal effects from self-selected surveys. Report disagreements, uncertainty and gaps. A future quantitative synthesis would require comparable effect sizes and a separate plan.
